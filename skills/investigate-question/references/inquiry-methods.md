# Search, reading, and synthesis methods

## Search design

Start with domain terms, synonyms, mechanisms, outcomes, and foundational sources. For a landscape, search relevant approach families and follow references backward and citations forward when available. For current tools, inspect official documentation and version details. Respect a supplied-corpus boundary and identify gaps needing outside evidence.

Record query/source, date, inclusion rationale, versions, and inaccessible material. A narrative scan is not a systematic review: exhaustive coverage requires a suitable protocol, retrieval strategy, screening, and exclusion accounting. Do not imply that coverage from a few familiar sources is complete.

## Reading questions

- What problem, population, or workload is actually addressed?
- What mechanism changes relative to the closest relevant baseline?
- What assumptions, theorem conditions, implementation constraints, or data selection govern the claim?
- What does each result measure? Is the improvement absolute, relative, aggregate, conditional, or derived?
- Which negative cases, ablations, failure modes, and applicability limits are reported or missing?
- Which claims are established by evidence, and which are interpretation or future work?

Use page/section/figure/table locations. When sources disagree, test whether terminology, workload, metric, baseline, or version explains the disagreement before concluding one is wrong.

## Synthesis patterns

| Need | Pattern |
| --- | --- |
| Explain a mechanism | Problem → baseline → changed mechanism → concrete example → assumptions → consequence. |
| Understand a field | Approach families → shared concepts → disputed assumptions → evidence clusters → gaps and promising questions. |
| Compare candidates | Eligibility conditions; evidence under comparable conditions; operating tradeoffs; unknowns. |
| Recommend direction | Recommendation → conditions → supporting/contrary evidence → confidence → what would reverse it. |
| Continue open study | Learning delta → revised concept map → live questions → next branches and resource implications. |

Adapt the narrative to the current question. A continuing study benefits from a structured ledger; an explanation need not reproduce the ledger. Preserve why an idea was rejected to prevent repeating the same investigation later.

The final synthesis must let a human inspect how the findings follow, what credible alternatives remain, what is unresolved, and what decision or investigation is justified next. Explain disagreements and evidence limits rather than ending with an annotated source list or a status notice. When empirical work was requested, report its executed evidence separately from published results and recommendations; unresolved execution belongs in the outstanding scope. Preserve useful open questions without manufacturing a product decision or treating every unexplored branch as a defect.
